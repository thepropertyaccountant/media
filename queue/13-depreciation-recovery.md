# 13 — The depreciation you claimed comes back when you sell

**Audience:** residential rental owners. **Suggested:** bank — no fixed slot
**Source guide:** guides/rental-property-deductions.html
**Why now:** no seasonal hook, which is why it sits in the bank. Deploy it
whenever a slot needs filling. It pairs with post 03 (the chattels schedule),
which sells the deduction; this one shows the bill at the other end, so run it
some weeks after 03 rather than straight after it.

## Verify before building
This post asserts a mechanism rather than a list of rates, so verify the
mechanism carefully — getting it half-right would be worse than not posting.
- VERIFY: the core rule — where a depreciable asset is sold for more than its
  **adjusted tax value**, the excess is **depreciation recovery income** and is
  taxable in the year of sale, capped at the depreciation previously claimed
  (i.e. recovery is limited to original cost; gain above original cost is not
  recovery income). Confirm each limb —
  https://www.ird.govt.nz/income-tax/income-tax-for-businesses-and-organisations/types-of-business-expenses/depreciation/managing-depreciation
  and IR260 "Depreciation — a guide for businesses",
  https://www.ird.govt.nz/-/media/project/ir/home/documents/forms-and-guides/ir200---ir299/ir260/ir260.pdf
- VERIFY: the opposite case — a **loss on disposal** where the asset sells for
  less than adjusted tax value, and whether it is deductible. Confirm before
  stating it; do not assume it mirrors the recovery rule.
- VERIFY: how this applies when a rental is **sold as a whole**, with chattels
  included in one price — whether and how the sale price must be apportioned
  across the chattels, and what happens where the agreement is silent. Check
  IR264 and IR361 "Tax and your property transactions",
  https://www.ird.govt.nz/-/media/project/ir/home/documents/forms-and-guides/ir300---ir399/ir361/ir361.pdf
  **This is slide 4 and the sharpest point in the post. If the apportionment
  position cannot be confirmed, cut that slide rather than soften it.**
- VERIFY: that the building itself is still not depreciable, so recovery is a
  chattels question for residential rental, not a building one — IR264.
- VERIFY: whether any purchase price allocation rules apply to the apportionment
  between land, building and chattels on sale, and whether vendor and purchaser
  must adopt the same allocation. If this is in force it belongs in the post;
  confirm it properly before naming it.
- No rates are needed. Any dollar figures on slide 5 are an illustration, not a
  tax figure — label them as an example.

## Caption
Hook: `The depreciation you claimed on the rental's chattels was never a gift. Selling can bring some of it back.`

Then: every year you depreciate the carpet, the appliances and the heat pump,
their book value drops while the things themselves keep some real worth. Sell
them for more than that written-down value and the difference is depreciation
recovery income, taxable in the year you sell rather than spread back across the
years you claimed it. The place it ambushes people is a whole-property sale at
one price, because the chattels are still in there and the question of what they
sold for does not disappear because the agreement said nothing about it. None of
this makes the chattels schedule a bad idea — the deductions along the way are
usually worth considerably more than the recovery at the end. It just means the
number is worth knowing before you agree a price, not after.
