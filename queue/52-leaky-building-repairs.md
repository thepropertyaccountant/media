# 52 — Leaky building repairs can be deductible. The upgrade is not.

**Audience:** residential rental owners, Auckland especially. **Status: BANK**
**Best deployed:** any time. Leave a few weeks between this and post 26
(repairs versus improvements) and post 39 (body corporate levies).
**Source guide:** guides/rental-property-deductions.html
**Why it works:** an Auckland problem with very large numbers attached, and
Inland Revenue has published an example on each side of the line.

## Verify before building
**Read both examples in full before building. This draft was written from a
summary of them, and the amounts at stake for a reader are large. If the
examples turn on facts the slides do not reflect, rewrite the slides to match
or skip the post.**
- VERIFY Interpretation Statement IS 12/03, "Deductibility of repairs and
  maintenance expenditure - general principles", Examples 12 and 13 —
  https://www.taxtechnical.ird.govt.nz/-/media/project/ir/tt/pdfs/interpretation-statements/is-1203.pdf
  Last known: Example 12, a leaky home repaired like for like, was revenue
  (deductible). Example 13, re-clad with a superior system, was capital.
- VERIFY the general test the examples apply: work that results in the
  "reconstruction, replacement or renewal of the asset, or substantially the
  whole of the asset" is capital, as is work "that has the effect of changing
  the character of the asset". Check how much of the building was affected in
  Example 12 — if the deductible result depends on the damage being limited,
  slide 2 must say so.
- VERIFY the statement is still current (it showed a 2023 modification date).
- **DO NOT STATE** that remediation is deductible, or capital, as a general
  rule. The post says Inland Revenue has an example of each and that most real
  jobs are a mix.
- **DO NOT STATE** how the split between repair and upgrade is to be
  calculated. Slide 5 says it needs working out and stops there.
- Slides 5 and 6 are practical observation and advice, not stated rules.

## Caption
Hook: `Repairing a leaky rental can be deductible. Upgrading it while you are there is not, and most remediation does both.`

Then: Inland Revenue's statement on repairs includes two leaky-building
examples. In one, the damage is repaired like for like and the cost is a
deductible repair. In the other, the building is re-clad in a superior system
and the cost is capital. The line is the ordinary one: a repair restores what
was there and an improvement makes it better. The difficulty is that real
remediation is rarely one or the other. What failed usually cannot go back
exactly as it was, so a job contains both repair and upgrade. With sums this
size the split matters, and it is far easier to establish from an itemised
scope of works before the contract is signed than from a single invoice
afterwards.
