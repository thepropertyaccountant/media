# 24 — Your rental can lose cash and still owe tax

**Audience:** residential rental owners. **Status: BANK**
**Best deployed:** any time. Lands well between January and March when owners
are looking at the year's bank statements.
**Source guide:** guides/rental-property-deductions.html
**Why it works:** the most common confusion in a rental return. The owner sees
money going out every fortnight and assumes a loss. Pairs with post 02
(ring-fencing) but is a different point.

## Verify before building
- VERIFY: principal repayments are not deductible. IR264 lists "the capital
  portion of any mortgage repayments" among what cannot be deducted —
  https://www.ird.govt.nz/-/media/project/ir/home/documents/forms-and-guides/ir200---ir299/ir264/ir264.pdf
  and the rental expenses page lists "the principal portion of mortgage
  repayments" —
  https://www.ird.govt.nz/property/renting-out-residential-property/residential-rental-income-and-paying-tax-on-it/rental-expense-deductions
- VERIFY: interest is **100% deductible from 1 April 2025** —
  https://www.ird.govt.nz/property/renting-out-residential-property/residential-rental-income-and-paying-tax-on-it/rental-expense-deductions/property-interest-rules
- No tax figures are asserted. The ledger on slide 3 is an illustration — label
  it as an example and **check the arithmetic at build time**:
  36,000 − 30,000 = 6,000 profit; 36,000 − 30,000 − 11,000 = −5,000 cash.

## Caption
Hook: `Your rental can cost you money every fortnight and still show a taxable profit.`

Then: a mortgage repayment is two things. The interest is a cost of earning the
rent and is deductible. The principal is you buying more of the house, and it
is never deductible, however much of it leaves your account. So a property can
be several thousand dollars short on cash for the year and still produce a
profit on the return, with tax to pay on it. It also gets worse with time,
because on a table mortgage each year's repayments carry less interest and more
principal. The practical step is small: use the interest figure from the bank's
annual loan summary, not the total of the payments, and forecast the tax on
that basis rather than on what the account balance did.
